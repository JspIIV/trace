The Silent Economics of Aging Bridges

Every aging bridge is a promise made by people who are mostly gone, paid for a
second time by people who never voted on it. A bridge does not announce its
decline. It carries the same trucks it carried last year, and the year before,
until one cold morning an inspector finds a crack that has been widening in the
dark for a decade. The cost of that crack was always there; it was simply unbilled.

What makes bridges difficult is that maintenance is invisible when it works. A beam
repainted on schedule looks, to everyone who drives across it, exactly like a beam
that is quietly rusting through. The reward for doing the work well is that nothing
happens, and nothing happening is the least persuasive case a budget can make. So
the money goes to the ribbon-cuttings, and the ledger of deferred repairs grows in
a column no one reads until it becomes a headline.

The honest way to run a bridge is to treat the boring years as the important ones.
